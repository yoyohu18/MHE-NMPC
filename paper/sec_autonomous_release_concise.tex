\subsection{Eventless grasp--release lifecycle}\label{sec:autorelease}

The controller issues the gripper-release command itself, but deliberately does not use that
command to clear its payload model: a commanded release is not proof of physical detachment.
We compare an event-driven operational reference (arm~A), which resets payload geometry and
inner-loop scaling with the command, against the proposed interface (arm~B), which consumes
only healthy, fresh $m/s/J$ frames. Arm~B completes a release only after its no-payload
confidence remains above 0.90 for \SI{1.0}{s}. The two arms were paired under three payload and
trajectory conditions. A pre-registered pair was voided and re-flown if either arm crashed
before release or lacked payload evidence; these exclusions are reported below and are not
absorbed into the completion result.

Among valid pairs, arm~B completed autonomous release confirmation in 22/22 flights with no
observed post-release instability (Table~\ref{tab:complete}). The pooled median delay was
\SI{2.70}{s}, including the \SI{1.0}{s} confidence hold. The two-sided \SI{95}{\percent}
Clopper--Pearson interval is $[\SI{84.6}{\percent},\SI{100}{\percent}]$, so this result
demonstrates closed-loop feasibility rather than a zero failure rate. In all 22 flights the
payload-presence machine made exactly two transitions---one autonomous load declaration and
one autonomous empty declaration---with no carrying-phase transition. The published first
moment decayed continuously rather than stepping to zero, confirming that recovery used the
intended estimator interface rather than the controller's release command.

\paragraph{Safety boundary}
The 22/22 batch validates the interface once its release evidence is accepted; it does not
validate a universally robust detector. We therefore tested three physically distinct evidence
sources: payload quantity, directional thrust residual, and collapse of the normalized first
moment. A fast branch combining quantity and residual without moment evidence declared release
prematurely in 3/9 randomized flights during a \SI{4}{m/s} figure-eight. Loaded-flight mass
estimates could remain below the nominal empty threshold for up to \SI{48}{s}, while a dynamic
thrust excursion reached \SI{-1.69}{N}, exceeding the \SI{-1.47}{N} weight change of the
smallest payload. Neither source is therefore reliable on its own in this regime.

We rejected that branch and require every release decision to include fresh, successful
moment-collapse evidence. Ambiguous cases enter \textsc{unresolved}; a timeout does not clear
the model or reset adaptive state. In a subsequent 12-flight smoke test, no flight declared
release before the command: 11 releases were confirmed with a median detector delay of
\SI{1.9}{s}, and one remained safely unresolved (Table~\ref{tab:release-safety}). This is the
supported safety conclusion; broader detector robustness remains outside the present claim.

The paired performance comparison is conditional on successful attachment and survival to the
release command. Of 76 flights, 18 were voided and re-flown: 15 crashed before release and three
lacked MHE payload evidence. The available logs cannot distinguish physical capture failure
from estimator non-recognition in those three cases. Among valid pairs, steady tracking RMS
differed by at most \SI{0.8}{mm} between arms; no degradation of engineering significance was
observed, although no equivalence test was pre-registered. The measurable cost of refusing the
event shortcut is the \SIrange{2.6}{3.0}{s} median confirmation delay.
