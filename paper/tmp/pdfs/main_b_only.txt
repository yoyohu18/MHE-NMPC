Eventless Payload-Adaptive NMPC for Quadrotors
Anonymous Authors
Affiliation withheld for double-anonymous review

Abstract—A release command does not guarantee physical
payload detachment, so switching a controller’s model from
an attach/drop signal can be unsafe. We present an eventless
payload-adaptive control framework in which a moving horizon
estimator (MHE) [1] uses only motor speeds and odometry
to publish an atomic, health-gated frame of composite mass,
first mass moment s = mP rxy , and inertia to an outer-loop
nonlinear model predictive controller (NMPC). The first-moment
representation remains well defined as the payload vanishes,
while bounded filtering and continuous body-rate scheduling
prevent discontinuous model updates. In PX4 [2] SITL with
physically attached eccentric payloads, the interface completes
release confirmation in 31/31 eligible eventless flights with no
observed post-release instability, at a median delay of 3.46 s when
confirmation clears in maneuver and 16.48 s when a brake to
hover is required first. A safety ablation shows payload quantity
and thrust residual to be individually unreliable in aggressive
flight—their fast branch cleared the model prematurely in 3/9
flights; restricting the detector to fresh moment-collapse evidence
removed every premature declaration over 12 flights. Against an
L1-adaptive [3] NMPC baseline, the proposed method provides
comparable transient tracking, recovers faster (L1 lags by 8 %
to 32 %), and retains interpretable physical parameters. These
results demonstrate the eventless control interface and its failsafe semantics, while not claiming distribution-wide robustness
of the release detector.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Autonomous aerial delivery and in-flight aerial manipulation
subject a multirotor to abrupt changes in its own dynamics.
Grasping or releasing a parcel changes three coupled quantities
at once: the total mass m (which rescales the thrust-toacceleration map and the hover throttle), the center of mass
(a thrust that no longer passes through the composite CoM
produces a constant trim torque τ ≈ ∆r × f , significant even
for small offsets because f ≈ mg is large), and the rotational
inertia (which slows the attitude response and effectively
lowers the inner-loop bandwidth). The first channel is an outerloop (position/thrust) problem; the latter two are inner-loop
(attitude/rate) problems. A controller that models the payload
only as a scalar mass will mistrack or, for sufficiently heavy
eccentric loads, destabilize.
Explicit parameter estimation (EKF/MHE of mass, CoM,
inertia) is interpretable and can feed model-based constraints,
but converges slowly across an abrupt change; lumpeddisturbance adaptation (L1-adaptive NMPC, INDI) reacts
fast but yields no physical model; learning-based estimators
typically need rich sensing or ground-truth data.
The central question of this work is whether the complete
grasp–carry–release lifecycle can adapt its payload model
without trusting a commanded or externally supplied payload
event. Our answer rests on two observations. First, payload

mass, first moment, and inertia can be estimated continuously
without consuming a gripper command, attachment state, or
external event notification. Second, motor speeds are a free
physical sensor: both collective thrust and body torques can
be reconstructed from rotor speeds alone, yielding physical
residuals that are decoupled from the estimator’s own state.
Combining the two yields a sensing-minimal estimator that
needs no external event signal and no measurement of the
attachment geometry.

II. R ELATED W ORK
Agile quadrotor flight. Geometric tracking on SE(3) [4]
and time-optimal flight [5] assume known, fixed inertial parameters, which a payload event invalidates mid-flight.
Aerial manipulation and transport. Aerial robots that
grasp, carry and release objects are surveyed in [6]; dynamic
aerial grasping, with explicitly modeled gripper dynamics, was
demonstrated in [7]. Load transportation, by grasping or cable
suspension, is surveyed in [8]. Ours is the transport-and-release
subproblem: the payload is rigidly attached, so the controller
must handle the resulting change in mass, inertia and centre
of mass.
Control under changing payload. Adaptive and robust
designs—L1-adaptive NMPC [9], INDI [10]—treat the load
as a lumped disturbance and are the standard baselines for
payload-carrying flight; L1-NMPC in particular reports large
tracking-error reductions under unknown load on aggressive
trajectories. A parallel line learns the residual dynamics instead: [11] fits unmodeled aerodynamic effects with Gaussian
processes inside the MPC, which improves tracking without
yielding a named physical parameter either. Slung-load control

addresses the swinging-payload case explicitly [12], exploiting
differential flatness of the cable-suspended system; a rigidly
attached payload instead changes the vehicle’s own inertial
parameters. A related proprioceptive line estimates the external
wrench directly with momentum-based observers, enabling
collision detection and interaction control [13]. All of these
recover a force/torque, not a physical parameter: they cannot,
e.g., update a thrust-margin constraint or an inner-loop gain
schedule from the estimate.
MHE and online parameter estimation. Filter- and
optimization-based estimators recover mass, inertia, and geometric parameters [14]–[16]. Standard MHE performance is
highly sensitive to the process/measurement weight matrices;
neural MHE (NeuroMHE) [17] learns to output these weights
online, resolving the bias–variance trade-off but estimating a
lumped disturbance force rather than explicit named parameters.
Residual-based
change
detection. Residual-based
fault/change detection (CUSUM and related methods [18])
motivates the evidence checks used by the payload lifecycle
state machine. Our positioning: we do not learn dynamics
and add no sensing; every deployed signal derives from
motor speeds and odometry. This is a deliberate departure
from learning-based MPC [19] and the broader safe-learning
agenda [20], where a learned component absorbs the
model mismatch; here the mismatch is resolved into named

No-payload confidence is the product of smooth empty
scores for payload quantity, first moment, and inertia. A
nonzero moment therefore vetoes a false empty decision even
if the mass estimate touches its lower bound. On accepted
release evidence, the estimator switches only the published
target of s to zero; the optimization state is not overwritten.
The output filter consequently produces a continuous decay
rather than a geometric step. The controller marks a planned
drop complete, resets its L1 state, and finishes recovery only
after healthy confidence remains above 0.90 for 1.0 s; issuing
the gripper command itself does none of these things.
B. Why the first mass moment is the essential state
Estimating CoM offset alone is ill-conditioned at release:
physically s = mP rxy → 0, whereas rxy itself is undefined as
mP → 0. In an earlier mass-only formulation, the optimizer
could suppress a phantom eccentricity only by driving total
mass below the bare-airframe value; 14 of 55 post-release
samples then pinned at the lower bound. Augmenting s
supplies the rotational channel directly and permits cxy = s/m
without knowing the grasp-dependent horizontal offset. A
frozen-window inertia coefficient prevents the optimizer from
using instantaneous mass as a free inertia knob, while the
weak rz prior retains the dominant parallel-axis term. This
structure is the minimum one that simultaneously represents
payload existence, trim moment, and roll/pitch inertia without
an external attachment geometry measurement.
The release detector studies three physically distinct
sources: (i) collapse of ρ = ∥s∥/∥s∥ref below 0.30, where
the loaded reference is formed from a healthy, low-dynamic,
in-envelope interval and frozen before aggressive flight, with
an absolute ∥s∥ bound used only as a sanity check; (ii)
payload quantity, in which mass and algebraically derived
inertia count as one source; and (iii) a directional thrustresidual edge with finite validity. Within this unified detector a
single source never releases. The slow path requires quantityempty and moment-collapse evidence for five MHE frames.
The release-only residual is deliberately separate from the
4.4 N schedule-confirmation threshold and uses a signed twowindow difference, ∆T = T post − T pre , with 0.3 s half-

windows. We evaluated two residual branches: fastA paired
a normal 0.9 N residual with quantity-empty evidence, while
fastB paired a longer-persistent residual with moment collapse.
A recorded figure-eight transient reached −1.69 N, larger in
magnitude than the −1.47 N step of the minimum supported
payload, so amplitude alone cannot separate the cases. Online
and offline evaluation call the same pure decision function.
The randomized smoke of Sec. VI-A rejects fastA; the unified
detector therefore fires only on moment-collapse evidence
(slow or fastB) and treats an unresolved timeout as unknown,
never as proof of an empty vehicle. Two further routes remain
in the final interface and are reported with their frequencies in
Sec. VI-A: a mass-domain path counted only in low-dynamic
flight, and controller confirmation on the published no-payload
confidence. Moment evidence must also originate from a fresh,
successful MHE solve: the re-anchoring routine deliberately
initializes s to zero after five consecutive solver failures, so
that initialization is invalid evidence rather than a physical
collapse.

Real-time budget. Both optimizers are acados [23] SQP
solvers with merit backtracking and the HPIPM [24] QP
solver. On one desktop core shared with Gazebo/PX4 SITL,
the deployed 13-state NMPC (N = 20, 20 Hz) solves in 1.3 ms
at the median and 2.2 ms at the 99th percentile (n = 1009),
i.e. 2.6 % and 4.4 % of its 50 ms period. The deployed 16-state
first-moment MHE (N = 20, 10 Hz) solves in 9.2 ms at the

with a 72.8 ms maximum (n = 3740, first 20 frames per flight

deadline in simulation, but the tail margin is narrow, so we
make no onboard-headroom claim; that requires measurement
on the target computer. Setpoints are published at 50 Hz by
interpolating the latest prediction.

VIII. C ONCLUSION
We presented an eventless payload-adaptive NMPC interface driven by motor speeds and odometry. An atomic, healthgated m/s/J frame supports continuous model and body-rate
adaptation without treating release commands as detachment
evidence. The interface completed 31/31 eligible eventless
SITL flights without observed post-release instability, conditional on attachment and pre-release survival. The safety
ablation rejects quantity-plus-residual clearing without moment evidence; the final batch also exercised mass-domain and
confidence-only confirmation paths. These results demonstrate
interface feasibility, not a universally robust detector. Reliable
moment-reference formation and hardware validation remain
open.
