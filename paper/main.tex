\begin{abstract}
A release command does not guarantee physical payload detachment, so switching a controller's
model from an attach/drop signal can be unsafe. We present an eventless payload-adaptive control
framework in which a moving horizon estimator (MHE) uses only motor speeds and odometry to publish
an atomic, health-gated frame of composite mass, first mass moment $s=m_P r_{xy}$, and inertia to
an outer-loop nonlinear model predictive controller (NMPC). The first-moment representation
remains well defined as the payload vanishes, while bounded filtering and continuous body-rate
scheduling prevent discontinuous model updates. A thrust-residual self-trigger deweights stale
pre-change stages in the MHE window and reduces mass-estimate settling time by up to
\SI{4}{\times}. In PX4 software-in-the-loop experiments with physically attached eccentric
payloads, the eventless interface completes release confirmation in 22/22 valid paired flights,
with no observed post-release instability and a median confirmation delay of \SI{2.70}{s}.
A safety ablation shows that payload quantity and thrust residual are individually unreliable
during aggressive flight: their fast release branch causes premature model clearing in 3/9
flights. Requiring fresh moment-collapse evidence eliminates premature declarations in a
12-flight smoke test, yielding 11 confirmations and one conservative unresolved outcome.
Against an L1-adaptive NMPC baseline, the proposed method provides comparable transient tracking,
recovers \SIrange{15}{35}{\percent} faster, and retains interpretable physical parameters. These
results demonstrate the eventless control interface and its fail-safe semantics, while not
claiming distribution-wide robustness of the release detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Autonomous aerial delivery and in-flight aerial manipulation subject a multirotor to abrupt
changes in its own dynamics. Grasping or releasing a parcel changes three coupled quantities
at once: the total \emph{mass} $m$ (which rescales the thrust-to-acceleration map and the
hover throttle), the \emph{center of mass} (a thrust that no longer passes through the
composite CoM produces a constant trim torque $\tau \approx \Delta r \times f$, significant
even for small offsets because $f \approx mg$ is large), and the \emph{rotational inertia}
(which slows the attitude response and effectively lowers the inner-loop bandwidth). The
first channel is an outer-loop (position/thrust) problem; the latter two are inner-loop
(attitude/rate) problems. A controller that models the payload only as a scalar mass will
mistrack or, for sufficiently heavy eccentric loads, destabilize.

Three families of methods address load changes. \emph{Explicit parameter estimation}
(EKF/MHE of mass, CoM, inertia) is interpretable, integrates natively with a model-based
controller, and can update thrust-margin constraints, but classical estimators converge
slowly across an abrupt change because the estimation window or filter averages across the
discontinuity. \emph{Lumped-disturbance adaptation} (L1-adaptive NMPC, INDI) compensates the
aggregate effect without estimating physical parameters, reacting fast but yielding no
interpretable model. \emph{Learning-based} methods (e.g.\ neural MHE that regresses estimator
weights) can resolve the estimator's bias--variance trade-off but typically require rich
sensing or offline ground-truth data.

The central question of this work is whether the complete grasp--carry--release lifecycle can
adapt its payload model without trusting a commanded or externally supplied payload event.
Our answer rests on two observations. First, an abrupt payload change leaves a detectable
common-mode signature in reconstructed collective thrust, so the estimator can discover
\emph{when} the dynamics changed without consuming a gripper command, attachment state, or
external event notification. Second, \textbf{motor speeds are a free physical sensor}: both
collective thrust and body torques can be reconstructed from rotor speeds alone, yielding
physical residuals that are decoupled from the estimator's own state. Combining the two
yields an estimator that is both fast (it deweights stale pre-event measurements the moment
the thrust residual crosses a threshold) and \emph{sensing-minimal} (it needs no external
event signal and no measurement of the attachment geometry).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An \textbf{eventless payload-adaptive NMPC architecture} whose model never consumes an
attach/drop notification or treats its own release command as evidence. An atomic, health-gated
$m/s/J$ frame drives bounded model updates and continuous body-rate scheduling
(\S\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:method}).
\item A \textbf{self-triggered MHE} that detects abrupt payload changes from reconstructed
thrust and deweights stale pre-event window stages, reducing mass-estimate settling time by up
to ${\sim}4\times$ without an external event signal. The first-moment state
$s=m_P r_{xy}$ represents eccentricity without measured attachment geometry and remains
well-defined at release (\S\ref{sec:method}; \S\ref{sec:results}-A).
\item \textbf{Closed-loop lifecycle and safety validation}: 22/22 valid paired flights complete
eventless release without observed post-release instability. A targeted ablation rejects an
unsafe quantity-plus-residual branch and shows that fresh moment-collapse evidence with an
\textsc{unresolved} fail-safe state is necessary (\S\ref{sec:autorelease}).
\end{enumerate}
The runtime estimator consumes only motor speeds and odometry; the sole geometric quantity not
identified online is a weak vertical-arm prior $r_z$, which is structurally unobservable from
the thrust-moment channel.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Control under changing payload.} Adaptive and robust designs---L1-adaptive NMPC
\cite{hanover2021}, INDI \cite{smeur}---treat the load as a lumped disturbance and are the
standard baselines for payload-carrying flight; L1-NMPC in particular reports large
tracking-error reductions under unknown load on aggressive trajectories. Slung-load control
addresses the swinging-payload case explicitly. These methods do not produce a physical
parameter and cannot, e.g., update a thrust-margin constraint from the estimate.

\textbf{MHE and online parameter estimation.} Filter- and optimization-based estimators
recover mass, inertia, and geometric parameters \cite{svacha2020,wuest2019,bohm2021}.
Standard MHE performance is highly sensitive to the process/measurement weight matrices;
neural MHE (NeuroMHE) \cite{neuromhe} learns to output these weights online, resolving the
bias--variance trade-off but estimating a lumped disturbance force rather than explicit named
parameters.

\textbf{Event-triggered and change detection.} Residual-based fault/change detection (CUSUM
and related) underlies our self-trigger. Our positioning: we do not learn dynamics and add no
sensing; every deployed signal derives from motor speeds and odometry. We separately test,
as a supporting ablation, whether numerical tuning improves the handcrafted transient-period
schedule.

\section{System Overview and Problem Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).} Three layers: Gazebo physics (with a magnetic
DetachableJoint gripper producing genuine inertia/CoM changes), the PX4 inner-loop rate
controller, and a ROS~2 outer loop (NMPC + MHE). The outer loop publishes a collective thrust
and a body-rate setpoint; PX4 tracks the body rate.

\textbf{Dynamics and parameterization.} The NMPC model carries the runtime parameter vector
$p = [x_{\text{ref}}, m, \Delta J, c_{xy}]$ (extended with a lumped disturbance only for the
L1 baseline). Thrust acting at the geometric center about the composite CoM produces
$\tau_{\text{com}} = [-c_y T, +c_x T, 0]$. The deployed MHE augments both composite mass and
the horizontal first mass moment,
\begin{equation}
x_{\mathrm{MHE}}=[x;m;s_x;s_y]\in\mathbb{R}^{16},\qquad s=m_P r_{xy},
\end{equation}
with $\dot m=0$ and $\dot s=0$ within each window ($N=20$, $\Delta t=\SI{0.1}{s}$). The
published geometry is reconstructed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
c_{xy}&=\frac{s}{m}, &
\mu&=\frac{m_B\max(m-m_B,0)}{m},\\
\Delta J_{xx}&=\Delta J_{yy}=\mu r_z^2.&&
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:continuous-geometry}
\end{equation}
where $r_z$ is a weak rack prior. The small $O(r_{xy}^2)$ diagonal terms and yaw increment are
omitted in the deployed interface. The hover-input baseline is recomputed from the live model
and includes the eccentric trim torque, avoiding a stale operating-point bias.

\textbf{Model-independent physical inputs.} We reconstruct the collective thrust
$T_{\text{phys}} = k \sum_i \omega_i^2$ and the body torques from rotor speeds and arm
geometry. Feeding the NMPC's \emph{commanded} input back into the estimator instead creates a
self-consistent blind spot: at hover $m \approx T/g$ is self-consistent for any $m$, so the
mass is unobservable from the commanded thrust. The motor-speed reconstruction breaks this
because it is decoupled from the controller/estimator state.

\textbf{Thrust-to-throttle inversion.} The offboard collective-thrust command is inverted to a
normalized rotor command through the motor model $T = k\omega^2$; because $k$ appears on both
the forward and inverse paths, steady-state tracking is independent of the absolute thrust
calibration.

\textbf{Continuous inner-loop scheduling.} Model consistency in the NMPC cannot by itself
restore the PX4 rate loop's lost bandwidth. The deployed path therefore maps the live inertia
increment to a body-rate-command scale, low-pass filters it, applies target hysteresis and
asymmetric rise/recovery slew limits, and preserves \SI{5}{\percent} command headroom before
the final saturation. It does \emph{not} rewrite PX4 parameters. An attach-time inertia-only
bootstrap uses the rack's \SI{0.30}{kg} payload envelope for immediate safety, then fades out
after persistent coherent MHE evidence; neither payload mass nor $s$ is injected by this
bootstrap, and the drop path remains estimate-only.

\subsection{Deweighting mechanism}
Within the sliding window, upon a payload event we deweight the $R/Q$ blocks of the pre-event
stages (per-stage \texttt{cost\_set}), rather than softening the mass anchor $Q_0$ (which is
already soft and is not the bottleneck; the true bottleneck is the $\dot m = 0$ rigidity that
lets stale cross-discontinuity measurements pull the mass). Guardrails: the terminal
measurement never enters the cost, and the first solve after an event keeps nominal weights.

\subsection{No-signal self-trigger}
The detector arms only after the baseline has settled (a warmup gate of consecutive quiet
thrust-residual frames), then fires when $|T_{\text{phys}} - \text{baseline}|$ exceeds a
threshold for a persistence count of consecutive frames. The warmup gate rejects the takeoff
transient; the persistence count rejects gust pulses (broadband/transient) while catching a
sustained mass step. This same criterion is the false-trigger criterion characterized under
wind in \S\ref{sec:results}. This detector schedules the MHE window weights. Release
confirmation uses the multi-source logic below; the two roles must not be conflated, because a
threshold suitable for a \SI{0.30}{kg} schedule event can suppress the residual vote of a
\SI{0.15}{kg} release.

\subsection{Continuous payload interface and autonomous lifecycle}
The MHE publishes one atomic frame
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{P}=\{&m,s_x,s_y,c_x,c_y,\Delta J_{xx},\Delta J_{yy},\Delta J_{zz},\\
              &J_{xx},J_{yy},J_{zz},\gamma,h,a\},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is no-payload confidence, $h$ is a health bit and $a$ is solution age. Health
requires a successful latest solve and fresh odometry, motor-speed, and known-input streams.
The NMPC additionally checks local receive age; an unhealthy or stale frame freezes the last
accepted model and breaks the confidence persistence timer. Valid frames pass through a
low-pass filter and separate slew limits for $m$, $s$, and $\Delta J$ before every NMPC solve.

Payload presence is an estimator-owned state, deliberately distinct from the legacy flag that
means ``an external attach notification was received.'' A hysteretic \textsc{empty}$\leftrightarrow$
\textsc{loaded} machine uses sustained payload-mass evidence, with a directional residual as a
fast path, and a separate latch records that the present flight reliably entered
\textsc{loaded}. This latch prevents an empty-flight confidence value from consuming the later
drop confirmation and prevents a transient mass dip during a figure-eight from disarming the
true release path.

No-payload confidence is the product of smooth empty scores for payload quantity, first moment,
and inertia. A nonzero moment therefore vetoes a false empty decision even if the mass estimate
touches its lower bound. On accepted release evidence, the estimator switches only the
\emph{published target} of $s$ to zero; the optimization state is not overwritten. The output
filter consequently produces a continuous decay rather than a geometric step. The controller
marks a planned drop complete, resets its L1 state, and finishes recovery only after healthy
confidence remains above 0.90 for \SI{1.0}{s}; issuing the gripper command itself does none of
these things.

\subsection{Why the first mass moment is the essential state}
Estimating CoM offset alone is ill-conditioned at release: physically
$s=m_P r_{xy}\rightarrow0$, whereas $r_{xy}$ itself is undefined as $m_P\rightarrow0$. In an
earlier mass-only formulation, the optimizer could suppress a phantom eccentricity only by
driving total mass below the bare-airframe value; 14 of 55 post-release samples then pinned at
the lower bound. Augmenting $s$ supplies the rotational channel directly and permits
$c_{xy}=s/m$ without knowing the grasp-dependent horizontal offset. A frozen-window inertia
coefficient prevents the optimizer from using instantaneous mass as a free inertia knob, while
the weak $r_z$ prior retains the dominant parallel-axis term. This structure is the minimum one
that simultaneously represents payload existence, trim moment, and roll/pitch inertia without
an external attachment geometry measurement.

The release detector studies three physically distinct sources: (i) collapse of
$\rho=\lVert s\rVert/\lVert s\rVert_{\mathrm{peak}}$, with an absolute $\lVert s\rVert$ bound
used only as a sanity check; (ii) payload quantity, in which mass and algebraically derived
inertia count as \emph{one} source; and (iii) a directional thrust-residual edge with finite
validity. A single source never releases. The slow path requires quantity-empty and
moment-collapse evidence for five MHE frames. The release-only residual is deliberately
separate from the \SI{4.4}{N} schedule-confirmation threshold and uses a signed two-window
difference, $\Delta T=\overline{T}_{\mathrm{post}}-\overline{T}_{\mathrm{pre}}$, with
\SI{0.3}{s} half-windows. We evaluated two residual branches: fastA paired a normal
\SI{0.9}{N} residual with quantity-empty evidence, while fastB paired a longer-persistent
residual with moment collapse. A recorded figure-eight transient reached \SI{-1.69}{N}, larger
in magnitude than the \SI{-1.47}{N} step of the minimum supported payload, so amplitude alone
cannot separate the cases. Online and offline evaluation call the same pure decision function.
The randomized smoke of Sec.~\ref{sec:autorelease} rejects fastA; the safety-accepted logic
therefore requires moment-collapse evidence (slow or fastB) and treats an unresolved timeout as
unknown, never as proof of an empty vehicle. Moment evidence must also originate from a fresh,
successful MHE solve: the re-anchoring routine deliberately initializes $s$ to zero after five
consecutive solver failures, so that initialization is invalid evidence rather than a physical
collapse.

\section{Simulation Setup}\label{sec:sim}
PX4 SITL with Gazebo (Harmonic), an x500 quadrotor, and a magnetic DetachableJoint gripper
that produces genuine inertia and CoM changes.

\emph{Two payload-change mechanisms, by design.} The physical DetachableJoint gripper attaches
 a separate rigid body and therefore couples mass, inertia and CoM together; it carries every
system-level claim (Tables~\ref{tab:matrix}, \ref{tab:wind}, and
\ref{tab:complete}). Separately, a \emph{wrench-emulated} step---a virtual force injection that
changes the effective translational mass instantaneously, with no inertia or CoM change---is
used only where a controlled ablation demands it: isolating the event trigger's
estimation-layer benefit requires a mass discontinuity that is clean and repeatable, free of
the grasp-depth scatter and inertia mismatch that a physical attach necessarily introduces
(Table~\ref{tab:nosignal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mest} only). The supporting weight-schedule
ablation runs on the physical gripper, as do all baseline and wind results. The two mechanisms are
complementary, not interchangeable, and we keep their scopes disjoint: any conclusion that
needs genuine rigid-body coupling is drawn only from the physical experiments (see the
red-line note in \S\ref{sec:results}-A). The wrench mechanism's inability to change rotational
inertia is a documented simulator limitation, not a modeling choice; we state it and its
consequences in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

Task state machine: approach (position control)
$\rightarrow$ NMPC hand-off and warmup hover $\rightarrow$ controlled descent $\rightarrow$
active grasp enable $\rightarrow$ lift $\rightarrow$ figure-eight $\rightarrow$
phase-triggered release. We state the NMPC scope explicitly: it governs the grasp hover and
the post-event adaptation phase; the approach controller is not part of the claim.
Headless batch infrastructure with a GCS-heartbeat stand-in; the physical-effectiveness
timestamp $t = 0$ is defined by $T_{\text{phys}}$ crossing the baseline, and in-band is defined
as \SI{\pm0.08}{kg}.

\emph{Frozen release configuration.} In the evaluated implementation,
\texttt{c\_xy\_est\_enable} gates the update routine that contains both the legacy mass-domain
test and the unified call to \texttt{release\_decision()}. The node and generic launcher retain
\texttt{false} as a backward-compatible default, but all 13 release-evaluation batch drivers
explicitly set it to \texttt{true}. The reported detector results therefore apply to those
frozen configurations, not to an unparameterized node launch; without the override, no release
decision is evaluated and the controller reaches the conservative \textsc{unresolved} timeout.

\emph{Real-time budget.} Both optimizers are acados SQP-RTI (one real-time-iteration SQP step
per call, HPIPM QP solver). Table~\ref{tab:timing} reports measured wall-clock solve times

\subsection{Supporting schedule-tuning ablation}\label{sec:tuning-ablation}
We additionally tested whether the handcrafted transient schedule required numerical
optimization. A simulation-in-the-loop cross-entropy search embedded M0 in a five-parameter
family and evaluated 96 closed-loop episodes. The surviving candidate was then compared with
M0 in a pre-registered paired $2\times2$ factorial ($n=8$ per cell). Its median settling-time
gain was \SI{-0.05}{s} for a \SI{0.2}{kg} payload and \SI{0.00}{s} for a \SI{0.3}{kg}
payload (both non-significant); the test had \SI{97}{\percent} power against the larger
previously observed effect. We therefore retain M0. This ablation supports a deliberately
narrow conclusion: for the tested family, \emph{when} to discard stale stages matters more
than the precise weight magnitude. It is not a learning contribution of the deployed system.

All ten settling-time tests are non-significant (smallest $p=.055$) and the total effect is a
median of zero at both masses: \textbf{neither factor contributes a measurable gain}. This is
not a power problem---against the effect sizes previously attributed to $\theta^\star$
($-0.64$, $\SI{-0.40}{s}$) and the paired standard deviations measured here (0.407,
\SI{0.311}{s}), Cohen's $d_z$ is 1.57 and 1.29, giving \textbf{97\% and 87\% power} at $n=8$.
Repeating the \{M0, $\theta^\star$\} comparison at the earlier \SI{10}{Hz} solve rate ($n=8$,
32 runs) likewise reproduces no gain (\SI{0.2}{kg}: $\SI{+0.70}{s}$, $\theta^\star$
\emph{slower} and converging within the window in only 5/8 runs; \SI{0.3}{kg}: $\SI{-0.25}{s}$,
$p=.25$), while M0 itself returns to its historical value there (3.83 vs.\ \SI{3.89}{s})---the
apparatus reproduces; $\theta^\star$ does not.

Finally we resolve the one confound in the earlier held-out win: its M0 baseline used a
\SI{0.8}{N} confirmation threshold rather than the deployed \SI{1.5}{N}. A three-arm paired
replication at that same operating point (M0@\SI{1.5}{N}, M0@\SI{0.8}{N}, $\theta^\star$, the
two M0 arms byte-identical but for the threshold; $n=8$, 3-order Latin square, 24 runs, zero
divergence) finds the three \textbf{indistinguishable on settling} ($0.00$, $-0.00$,
\SI{0.00}{s}; all $p\ge.375$), but shows the \SI{0.8}{N} baseline to be the weaker one on the
control layer (pos.\ error peak $\SI{+0.104}{m}$, $p=.031$), with $\theta^\star$'s advantage
over it ($\SI{-0.091}{m}$, $p=.047$) \textbf{vanishing} against the deployed baseline
($\SI{+0.027}{m}$, n.s.). Part of the historical win is therefore attributable to the weaker
baseline rather than to the learned schedule. Confirmation delay orders strictly and
significantly with the threshold across the three arms (1.28, 2.24, \SI{2.43}{s}; 7/0, 7/0,
8/0), confirming the manipulated variable acts---and that it does not convert into a settling
gain.

\textbf{Interpretation and consequence.} $\theta^\star$ is not a \emph{worse} policy but one
statistically indistinguishable from the hand-designed rule. Since a displacement along all
five dimensions at once costs nothing measurable, the objective is evidently \textbf{flat over
a broad neighbourhood} of M0 rather than sharply peaked at a point the search missed---which is
equally why the search could not descend reliably and why little was lost by not descending. On
this task \emph{when} to reweight carries the benefit; \emph{how much} is weakly determined. We
accordingly retain M0 and claim no learned-scheduling contribution. Every quantitative result
used to compare weight schedules, including
Tables~\ref{tab:nosignal},~\ref{tab:matrix}, and~\ref{tab:wind}, uses M0 with the historical
\SI{1.5}{N} threshold. The later continuous-interface batch of
Sec.~\ref{sec:autorelease} also uses the M0 rhythm but normalizes its schedule threshold by the
\SI{0.30}{kg} rack envelope, giving \SI{4.4}{N}; it is not part of the learned-policy
comparison. The sole $\theta^\star$ exception is the qualitative legacy visualization of
Fig.~\ref{fig:b5}, recorded before this ablation concluded. The claim's boundary is the tested
schedule family, task, and settling objective; a richer policy class, a different objective,
or an environment admitting common random numbers may behave differently.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:limits}
All system-level evidence is presently SITL-only. Hardware migration replaces simulated motor
speeds with ESC telemetry and must re-identify thrust/torque coefficients, noise floors, timing,
and gripper compliance before free flight. The vertical arm $r_z$ is unobservable from the
thrust-moment channel and remains a weak rack prior; the $s_x$/pitch channel is noisier than
$s_y$/roll. The deployed diagonal inertia approximation also drops the small
$O(r_{xy}^{2})$ and yaw-inertia terms.

The 22/22 paired result shows that the first-moment output can drive complete NMPC recovery
without a drop event, but it does not establish distribution-wide detector robustness. The
targeted safety ablation identifies the operative boundary: payload quantity and thrust
residual can become simultaneously false-empty during dynamic flight, causing premature model
clearing in 3/9 flights when combined without moment evidence. The accepted logic therefore
requires fresh, healthy moment-collapse evidence and treats timeouts as \textsc{unresolved},
never as proof of an empty vehicle. After this correction, 12 flights produced no premature
declarations, 11 confirmations, and one safe unresolved outcome.

The remaining detector limitation is its moment reference. A running maximum can absorb
dynamic estimation excursions above the physically feasible envelope and thereby make an
ordinary loaded moment appear collapsed. Future validation will form this reference only from
a healthy, low-dynamic, in-envelope interval and freeze it before aggressive flight. Until that
revision is validated across broader conditions, our claim is an eventless, fail-safe control
interface rather than a fully robust autonomous release detector.

The current software also has a configuration-coupling defect: the historically named
\texttt{c\_xy\_est\_enable} parameter is the effective master switch for all release evidence.
Although every reported release batch explicitly enabled it, the bare default is
\texttt{false} and therefore falls through to \textsc{unresolved}. Before deployment, release
decision execution must receive its own explicit enable parameter (or be unconditional in the
eventless profile), with startup validation that rejects inconsistent configurations.

\emph{Selection and pre-release failures.} The main performance tables condition on successful
attachment and survival to the release command. Eighteen of 76 flights were voided; arm~A had
11/38 pre-release crashes and arm~B had 4/38, while arm~B alone had 3/38 failures of the
pre-registered MHE payload-evidence check. The historical logging recorded an attach request but
not the simulator joint-state acknowledgement, so those three cases cannot be separated into
physical capture failures versus estimator non-recognition.
Nine arm~A crashes concentrate in W4, leaving only six valid pairs there. Consequently the
paper makes no claim that the interface improves grasp, lift, or early figure-eight stability,
and the W4 evidence is weaker than W3/W5. Attachment observability and the pre-release attitude
instability require separate experiments rather than reinterpretation of the conditional drop
result.

A sustained vertical up/downdraft remains intrinsically confounded with a payload change on a
collective-thrust-only residual; the existing wind ablation characterizes this limitation but
does not remove it.

\emph{Simulator fidelity.} We record two known deviations. (i) gz-sim Harmonic provides no
supported way to change a link's inertial properties at runtime such that the DART integrator
adopts them: \texttt{SetComponentData<Inertial>} updates the ECM component only, so
introspection tools report the new value while the physics continues to integrate the load-time
mass (gz-sim issue \#2733). Our wrench mechanism therefore emulates a \emph{translational} mass
step by force injection and leaves the rotational inertia unchanged; accordingly it supports
only mass-channel claims, and every claim involving inertia, CoM, or eccentricity comes from the
physically attached DetachableJoint platform, where the change is genuine (\S\ref{sec:sim}).
(ii) The eccentric payload is modeled as rigidly attached; a real magnetic gripper admits small
compliance and, for a suspended load, pendulum modes this model does not reproduce. Neither
deviation affects the sensing-minimal claim, which concerns which signals the estimator consumes
rather than how the disturbance is generated.

\emph{On learning.} Our simulation-in-the-loop optimization of the weight schedule produced no
measurable gain (\S\ref{sec:results}-B), and we identify the binding constraint as the sample
economics of high-fidelity evaluation---${\sim}\SI{85}{s}$ per closed-loop episode with no
injectable random seed, hence no common random numbers, hence per-candidate noise on the order
of the effect being optimized. Approaches that do not share this constraint remain open:
supervised targets extracted per-frame from flight data (rather than one scalar loss per
episode), or a lower-fidelity differentiable environment for search with SITL reserved for
validation. We regard the negative result as delimiting \emph{where} learning can pay off in
this pipeline, not as evidence against learned estimator tuning in general.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented an eventless payload-adaptive NMPC architecture that uses motor speeds and odometry
to update mass, first mass moment, and inertia without consuming attach/drop notifications or
treating a release command as proof of physical detachment. Self-triggered MHE window
deweighting accelerates abrupt-change recovery, while the atomic, health-gated $m/s/J$ interface
and continuous body-rate scheduling connect the estimate safely to the controller. Conditional
on successful attachment and pre-release survival, the interface completed release in 22/22
paired SITL flights with no observed post-release instability. It also matched the transient
tracking of an L1-NMPC baseline while recovering \SIrange{15}{35}{\percent} faster and retaining
interpretable physical parameters.

The release-safety ablation defines the claim boundary. Payload quantity and thrust residual
became simultaneously false-empty during aggressive flight, so the accepted design requires
fresh moment-collapse evidence and preserves ambiguous cases as \textsc{unresolved}. This
policy produced zero premature declarations in the corrected 12-flight smoke, with 11 confirmed
releases and one safe unresolved outcome. The present evidence therefore demonstrates that the
payload-event signal can be removed from the adaptive control interface; it does not establish
a fully robust release detector. A frozen, robust moment reference, broader SITL validation,
and subsequent HITL and flight testing are the next steps.
